Ankara Metro 2053

Every line that is operating, being built or officially planned, completed in four phases by 2053, plus the lines proposed here: an M7 ring line through Etlik, Keçiören and Altındağ, M5 through the Hacettepe, Bilkent and ODTÜ campuses to Mamak, an M8 crosstown line from İvedik OSB to Söğütözü and Gölbaşı, a northern M9 line through Keçiören, seven light rail and tram lines, an Airport Express, a Gölbaşı–Beynam line and Başkentray to Kahramankazan and Elmadağ. Lines that will run as one share a number.

Ankaray keeps 3-car trains for its 90 m stations and runs every 2 minutes at peak; its signalling allows a train every 90 seconds. The new metro lines get 150 m platforms; M1, M2, M3, M4 and M7 run 6 cars, M5, M6, M8 and M9 4, and the Airport Express 2 cars with luggage racks. Metro trains are built from 2-car units, so they can be lengthened or shortened a unit at a time. Where a line has room to spare, it runs shorter trains more often rather than longer trains less often: the Çubuk, Gölbaşı, Çayyolu and Akyurt light rail lines run single vehicles, every 6, 4, 4 and 7.5 minutes, on platforms built for pairs, and the Sincan and Altındağ lines run pairs every 2 minutes. Lines are sized for the peak hour of a term-time weekday in October–December: in the 2025 counts those months ran about 17% above the year's daily average, weekdays run about 19% above an average day, and the peak hour takes 12% of a direction's daily riders. Capacities count seats plus 4 standing passengers per m², the comfortable peak this plan is sized for. The operators' nominal figures assume about 6 per m²: 924 for a 3-car Ankaray train and 1,610 for a 6-car metro train, against 680 and 1,240 here, about a third more. That difference is the margin left for busy days, events and growth beyond the high case; light rail, Başkentray and the cable lines have a similar margin. Every line stays within capacity even with every assumption at its high end; M7, M6 and M3 come closest, at 90–98%, so a train every 5 minutes on M3, 6-car trains on M6 and a train every 2 minutes on the loop are the first steps if riders come in higher. Light rail runs at up to 70 km/h on its own tracks with priority at junctions. M4, M5, M7 and M8 are built for 100 km/h, M3 for 140 km/h beyond Kuyubaşı, so the Airport Express takes about 19 minutes from Gar to Esenboğa. Times assume 1 m/s² acceleration and braking, 30-second stops (40 on commuter lines) and an 8% margin. Başkentray runs 6-car pairs of its 3-car units at peak. The funicular runs 2-car trains of 250 passengers, and the gondolas 10-person cabins every 15 seconds.

Intervals are per direction. Peak is 07:00–09:30 and 16:00–19:30 on weekdays; day is the rest of the day until 22:00; evening is the early morning and late evening. Each line's peak interval gives the most for riders and the city for the money: shorter waits and the trips they attract ($10 an hour, plus $1.20 a trip for fares and fewer car trips) against running costs ($3.50 a car-km) and extra trains, with limits: no line runs less often than today, no urban line has more than 7.5 minutes between trains at peak, light rail runs at most every 2 minutes, and every busiest section stays within capacity even with every assumption at its high end.

M5, M7 and the Ring each run every 5 minutes, so a train passes every 2.5 minutes on the loop. Ankaray runs every 2 minutes, M1 and M2 every 3, M6 and M9 every 4, M4 and M8 every 5, and M3 every 6. The Sincan and Altındağ light rail lines run every 2 minutes, the Gölbaşı and Çayyolu lines every 4, the Çubuk line every 6 and the Akyurt line every 7.5. The Airport Express runs every 7.5 minutes and the funicular every 5. Each Başkentray service runs every 12 minutes, so trains run every 4 minutes between Sincan and Kayaş. Lines run 06:00–00:00; on Friday and Saturday nights Ankaray, M1, M2 and the Ring run every 15 minutes, and the Airport Express runs every night every 30 minutes.

Seasons. In 2025 the metro and Ankaray carried 173.2 million passengers. The model is calibrated to whole-year counts, so the yearly figures already include the seasons. Use follows the academic year, since students are among the most frequent riders: October–December ran about 17% above the year's daily average and June–August about 20% below, when students leave and many residents head to the coast; the Eid holidays dip too. Part of the rise through 2025 may also be growth. So the timetable above runs from September to mid-June, and a summer timetable from mid-June to early September and in holiday weeks. It keeps every interval except Ankaray (every 2.5 minutes) and Başkentray (every 15 minutes per service), and runs shorter trains where the high case still fits: 4 cars on M1, M2, M3 and M4, 2 on M5, and fewer gondola cabins. That needs about 1,638 cars at peak instead of 2,061 and saves about $73 million a year in running costs.

This timetable needs 526 trains and about 2,060 cars at peak, and costs about $1.43 bn a year to run plus $0.46 bn a year for the fleet. Running M1 and M2 every 2.5 minutes would need 546 trains and about $0.05 bn a year more, for about 37,000 more trips a day.

Başkentray services

Başkentray runs as one line with two western ends, Kahramankazan and Kazan Soda Fabrikası, and through trains to Elmadağ. Three services, each every 12 minutes at peak and 20 in the day and evening, combine to a train every 4 minutes at peak and every 6–7 minutes in the day between Sincan and Kayaş, against every 15 minutes today. With frequent trains and changes to the metro at most stations, Başkentray becomes an interchange line as well as a commuter line.

Ankara grows from 5.91 million in 2025 to 6.06 million in 2030 on TÜİK's projection [12], then more slowly as Türkiye's population peaks around 2050 [12]: 6.57 million in 2053, 11% more than today. Growth is uneven. Each district follows its 2017–2025 trend against the city, fading over time [12]: by 2053 Akyurt grows about 73%, Gölbaşı 53%, Kahramankazan 32%, Pursaklar 27%, Sincan 22%, Altındağ and Çubuk 17%, Etimesgut 16%, Yenimahalle 12% and Mamak 10%, while Çankaya, Keçiören and Elmadağ stay about level. New housing fills first: Dodurga and Alacaatlı (about 50,000 residents) and OYAK's 6,214 homes in Yapracık (about 25,000) by the early 2030s, and TOKİ's homes at Turkuaz filling to about 28,000 residents [14]; the rest of each district's growth, or decline, spreads over its existing neighbourhoods. How it was estimated: residents within 1 km of each station come from TÜİK's neighbourhood populations [13], each spread over its mapped area (leaving out industrial estates and parks), grown as above and counted at the nearest station (a resident near both a metro or rail station and a tram, gondola or funicular stop is counted at the metro or rail station, unless they live within about 150 m of the stop, a 2-minute walk, and nearer it than to any station: then they start at the stop, make as many trips as they would at the metro station, and can still walk on to it when that is quicker): 4.61 million around the 2053 network. The 1.44 million people living 1–5 km from a station form its feeder area: 80% of them make 0.18 trips a day each (0.14–0.25) by timed feeder bus, against about 0.11 today. Park-and-ride at 22 outer and new-housing stations, about 9,000 spaces, gives about 3 trips a day per space. Esenboğa is assumed to grow from 14 million passengers in 2025 to about 37 million in 2053, with 25% of them (20–30%) and those meeting them going by rail.

A station-to-station model measures how many residents, jobs and students each station can reach, counting time on the train, waits, walking and 5 minutes for each change. Trips per resident grow with that reach to the power 0.5 (0.35–0.65), in line with how public transport use responds to service elsewhere, calibrated so that 2025 matches each line's count [1]. They also rise with density: with the residents, jobs and students around a station, to the power 0.25 against today's average (0.7–1.4 times), because dense areas have less parking and more within walking distance. Stations used mainly to change from buses or cars keep those riders in full, but their few walk-up riders use rail less. Use also follows how rail compares with the car door to door at peak: the car needs 3 minutes to reach, drives 1.32 times the straight-line distance at 20 km/h on short urban trips rising to 45 km/h on long ones, and needs 2–12 minutes to park depending on how dense the destination is; rail takes the route model's time plus 12 minutes on foot. Use moves in proportion to that time ratio against today's average (0.7–1.4 times), so lines that bypass congestion, such as M3 to the airport and the Gölbaşı light rail, do better. Trips per resident fall 1% for each 1% rise in a typical journey's time once waiting is counted. Each trip goes to a station in proportion to what is there and how quickly it can be reached, and riders spread over near-equal routes. Weekdays are 19% above the daily average. Journeys average 12.0 km, against 7.4 km today, and 1.68 boardings, so counting every boarding gives about 1.99 billion a year (1.64–2.36 billion), at 5,090 trips per km a day (3,440 with today's timetables). The station directory lists residents, feeder area and entries for every station.

Market check. In 2025 Ankara's buses carried 264.6 million passengers and its urban rail 201.5 million [1], about 39 rail trips per resident. Cities that built rail at scale carry far more: Madrid's metro about 100 trips per resident a year, Stockholm's close to 200, and Istanbul's grew from less than Ankara's in 2000 to 758 million trips in 2022 [11]. Ankara's low use today mostly reflects how little of the city rail reaches: even M1, which carries more than half of rail riders, has few places to go without a bus or a change. Today's stations together carry about 72% of M1's rate per resident. As the network grows, every station moves from its own rate today, and new stations from that average, to a 2053 level at which the whole network averages about 110% of M1's rate today (95–125% across the tests), rising in a straight line over time: about a quarter of the way by 2032, just under half by 2038 and 75% by 2046. Keçiören's stations, at about 17% of M1's rate today, and Başkentray's, at about 49%, rise most as connections open. From then on, use depends only on what each station serves and how rail compares with the car. That gives 1,187 million trips a year in 2053, about 181 per resident (149–215): between Madrid's and Stockholm's metros.

How to raise ridership

1. Frequent trains all day

  • Fit CBTC signalling to M1, M2 and Ankaray. Build M4 to M8 driverless from the start.
  • Run each line as often as its riders justify, with shorter trains rather than longer waits where riders are fewer: every 2–7.5 minutes at peak, with a train every 2.5 minutes on the M5 and M7 loop, 3–10 minutes in the day and 4–15 in the evening, a summer timetable with shorter trains, with Ankaray, M1, M2 and the Ring all night on Friday and Saturday and the Airport Express every night.
  • Run Başkentray every 4 minutes at peak and every 6–7 minutes in the day on its own commuter tracks.
  • Build the new lines with 150 m platforms, open them with 4-car trains and add cars as demand grows. Ankaray stays at 3 cars, as its stations are 90 m long, and runs every 2 minutes at peak, with signalling that allows 90 seconds if riders outgrow the forecast.

In the estimate: +0.89 M trips a day, more for Başkentray, which goes from every 15 minutes to every 4. Shorter waits matter most for short trips, which are most trips in the centre.

2. Feeder buses timed to trains

  • Redraw the EGO network as trunk and feeder. Buses that run alongside a metro line into Kızılay become feeders to the nearest station.
  • Feeders every 6–10 minutes at peak, timed to arrive a few minutes before trains leave at the outer hubs: Sincan OSB, Sincan, Batıkent, Koru, Yaşamkent, Dodurga, Akyurt, Gölbaşı Merkez, İlkbahar, Forum, Karakaya, Natoyolu, Kayaş, Esenboğa and Çubuk Merkez.
  • Feeders also stop at the stations in between, not only at the hubs, so every station picks up riders from the streets 1–5 km around it; park-and-ride at the outer stations and at the new-housing stops of Yaşamkent Siteler, Alacaatlı Kümeevler, Ahmet Taner Kışlalı (Sarnıç AVM) and Karakaya.
  • Bus stops at station entrances without crossing a road, so a change takes under 3 minutes.
  • Bus lanes on the main approach roads, so feeders keep to their timetable.

In the estimate: +0.17 M trips a day from feeder buses and park-and-ride, mostly from the 1.44 million residents 1–5 km from a station, and from Keçiören once its buses feed M2.

3. One fare for the whole journey

  • One fare per journey, with free changes within 90 minutes between bus, metro, light rail and Başkentray.
  • Daily and weekly caps, so frequent riders never pay more than a pass.
  • AnkaraKart, bank cards and phones accepted at every gate and on every bus.
  • A separate airport fare on the express, with a supplement for pass holders.

Assumed in the feeder figure. Without it, a bus-and-train trip costs more than driving for many households.

4. Short interchanges

  • Kızılay: direct passages between M1, Ankaray and M2 with no extra ticket gates.
  • Sıhhiye–Yenişehir: a covered underground link to Başkentray.
  • Gar: M2, M3, M6, the Airport Express, Başkentray and YHT trains under one concourse.
  • Söğütözü, Batıkent, Ehlibeyt, Mamak, ODTÜ Merkez and Kayaş: cross-platform or single-concourse designs, under 3 minutes between platforms.

Not counted separately. Mamak, Batıkent and Söğütözü each join three lines, so slow transfers there would hold every estimate above back.

5. Easy walks to stations

  • Entrances on both sides of wide roads such as Eskişehir Yolu, İstanbul Yolu and Konya Yolu, with lifts.
  • Lit, continuous pavements and safe crossings within 1 km of each station.
  • Covered bike parking and shared bikes and scooters at stations.
  • Park-and-ride, already at Koru (380 spaces), Milli Kütüphane and Macunköy, extended to about 9,000 spaces at outer and new-housing stations: OSB-Törekent, Sincan OSB, Yaşamkent, Yaşamkent Siteler, Dodurga, Alacaatlı Kümeevler, Ahmet Taner Kışlalı, Forum, Karakaya, İlkbahar, Gölbaşı, Natoyolu, Kayaş, Esenboğa Merkez, Çubuk, Beynam, Kahramankazan, Akyurt and Elmadağ.

Not counted. A good walk widens each station's real catchment toward the 1.2 km case, about 5.0 million residents in 2053.

6. Homes and jobs near stations

  • Higher density and mixed use within 500 m of stations, starting with large sites on M5, M7 and M8: the Marşandiz yard, Ostim, Siteler and Balgat.
  • Hospitals, universities and public offices sited on rail, as Etlik Şehir Hastanesi will be on M6 and Bilkent Şehir Hastanesi on M5.
  • Shops and services inside or next to stations.

Not counted beyond the growth above. Over 27 years, new development near stations raises the number of people the network serves, not just how often they ride.

7. Manage cars in the centre

  • Fewer and higher-priced parking spaces in Kızılay, Ulus and Söğütözü, with the revenue going to transit.
  • Bus priority at junctions on feeder roads.
  • Office parking caps in new developments near stations.

Not counted. Often the strongest lever in other cities, and the hardest politically.

8. Reliable information

  • Real-time arrivals in stations, at bus stops and in one app for bus, metro and rail.
  • One network map for every mode.
  • Lifts and escalators kept working, with status shown in the app.

Not counted. Measures 4–8 push the result toward the high end of the range.

Why this network

What it fixes

  • Lines that run as one share a number: M1 (Dodurga–Yaşamkent–Kızılay–Batıkent–Sincan OSB) and M2 (Forum–Keçiören–Kızılay–İlkbahar) cross the centre without a change.
  • Four crosstown lines avoid Kızılay: M7 (Sincan–Batıkent–Etlik–Siteler–Mamak–Natoyolu), M5 (Turgut Özal–Batıkent–Ümitköy–the campuses–Ayrancı–Mamak), M8 (İvedik OSB–Söğütözü–Balgat–Gölbaşı) and M9 (İvedik OSB–Şehitler–Kuzey Ankara–Başpınar). M5 and M7 form a loop with Ring trains every 5 minutes each way.
  • Four light rail lines feed the metro where a metro would cost too much: a loop round Altındağ, a line through southern Sincan, one from Ümitköy through Çayyolu to Alacaatlı and Dodurga, and one from Fuar to Akyurt.
  • Rail reaches areas without it: Altındağ and Mamak, Şentepe and Etlik, north-west Keçiören, Balgat and Dikmen, Cinnah and Aşıkpaşa, central and southern Sincan, Çayyolu, Alacaatlı and Dodurga, Pursaklar, Akyurt, Gölbaşı, Kahramankazan, Elmadağ and Çubuk.
  • The universities, both city hospitals, the courts and ministries and the industrial zones (Ostim, İvedik, Sincan OSB, Saray, TUSAŞ, ASELSAN) are on rail. The airport is on rail by 2031, with an Airport Express from Gar from 2038.
  • Başkentray becomes a cross-city line with a train every 4 minutes, changes to the metro at eleven stations, branches to Kahramankazan and Kazan Soda and through trains to Elmadağ.

What it brings

  • Reach. 4.6 million residents within a 15-minute walk of a station in 2053, against 1.4 million today, and 1.4 million more within reach by feeder bus or park-and-ride. From a typical home near a station, rail reaches about 3.9 million residents, jobs and students within 45 minutes, against 1.3 million today.
  • Riders. 3.25 million trips a day and 1,187 million a year in 2053, about six times today; 1.98 million a day already by 2038.
  • Traffic. If a quarter to 30% of the new trips would otherwise be made by car, that is 0.80–0.96 million fewer car trips each weekday and 2.4–2.9 billion fewer car-km a year, 10–14% of city driving. Rush-hour delay on roads beside the lines, now 112 hours a year per driver [16], could fall by 20–34% at first.
  • Time. Bus riders who switch save 15–25 minutes a trip: 135–220 million hours a year, worth $1.4–2.2 bn.
  • Safety. Less driving could mean 27–40 fewer road deaths a year, against 290 in 2025 [16].
  • Economy. 15,000–30,000 construction jobs a year for 27 years; firms reaching more workers, worth 0.2–1% of the city's output; and land values near stations that typically rise 5–20%, part of which can help pay for the lines.
  • Value for money. Phase 1 costs $1.50–2.25 to build per extra trip, phases 2–4 $2.95–4.55. Benefits come to about 80–100% of the costs, and more if homes and jobs grow near stations and car use is managed (levers 6 and 7).

Potential issues

Risks

  • Cost. $33–51 bn over 27 years, $1.2–1.9 bn a year on average and up to $1.3–2.0 bn in phase 2: 26–40% of the 2026 budget of the municipality, EGO and ASKİ together [16], so most must come from the national government. Rail projects have run 45% over budget on average [15], which would mean $48–74 bn.
  • Riders. Rail ridership has averaged 51% below forecast [15]. This estimate is checked against each line's 2025 count and against Madrid, Stockholm and Istanbul, but its low end is 977 million trips a year.
  • Growth. Ankara grows only 11% by 2053, and the centre barely at all. If the outer districts grow more slowly than their recent trends, the Gölbaşı, Çubuk, Akyurt, Yapracık and Kahramankazan lines lose most.
  • Running costs. $1.43 bn a year against $0.48–0.69 bn in fares leaves a gap of $0.7–1.0 bn a year. Trains and signalling are priced in euros or dollars, so a weaker lira raises costs.
  • Time. Ankara's last metros took 11–14 years from start to opening; each phase here allows 6–8.
  • Building. 533 km of new route means years of lane closures where stations are dug from the surface, as on Mevlana Bulvarı and Eskişehir Yolu.
  • Environment. Tunnels under the ODTÜ forest and Atatürk Orman Çiftliği. M8 to Gölbaşı Kuzey and the Gölbaşı light rail from Mogan run along the edge of the Gölbaşı special environmental protection area around Mogan lake, on land rather than across the water. Lines to the outer edges can pull building outward unless planning keeps it near stations.
  • Rents. Rising values near stations can push renters out of areas such as Mamak, Siteler and Balgat.
  • Changes. 0.68 changes per journey, with about 140,000–180,000 a day at Siteler and at Kızılay. Cutting bus and dolmuş routes back to feed stations means conflict with operators.
  • Climate. Until electricity is cleaner, the trains' power emits about as much CO₂ as the fewer car-km save.
  • Weak pieces. The Akyurt light rail adds about 7,000 journeys a day even with Akyurt growing to about 80,000 residents: $11–19 of construction per extra trip, against $2.95–4.55 for phases 2–4 as a whole. The Çubuk light rail adds about 17,000 at $6–9 a trip; the Gölbaşı light rail about 44,000 at $4–6, above the plan as a whole, even with its planned development held to the district's trend. The Yapracık light rail adds about 20,000 at $6–10, assuming the OYAK and TOKİ homes fill. The Etlik–Mamak tram adds about 17,000 at $4–7 and the Millî Kütüphane–Kuyubaşı tram about 12,000 at $7–12; both mostly serve transfers and short trips that the model undercounts. Both of the weaker lines can wait for evidence: the Çubuk light rail opens in phase 2 because the Ministry has planned it, but could follow M3's results. Single stations with fewer than about 1,500 entries a day include Kazan Soda, Akçaören, Bitik, Yıldırımbeyazıt, Fuar and Atatürk Orman Çiftliği, Köstence today, the new Alacaatlı, Bangabandhu, Akyurt Sanayi, Büğdüz and Mogan stops, and Atatürk and Cumhuriyet on the Keçiören gondola. Akçaören has almost no one living within 1 km and is worth building only if housing or industry is planned around it.

Design questions

  • M8 passes Atatürk Orman Çiftliği and the Beştepe presidential complex, and M5 crosses university land and the ODTÜ forest: both need deep tunnels, with stations and shafts agreed with the landowners.
  • M5's published 53-minute time for its planned section should be checked; 18.6 km with 9 stations should take about 22–25 minutes.
  • Proposed station names are indicative. Proposed stations sit at the neighbourhood or landmark they are named after; stations on official plans whose sites are not yet published (M4, M6, M3 beyond Pursaklar and the Çubuk light rail) are placed within about 1 km of their namesakes, in the published order and consistent with the published lengths. The M7 station between İvedik OSB and Kaletepe is named Yahyalar, as Karşıyaka lies 2.7 km away.
  • M9 is built to a lighter standard: a tunnel only through central Keçiören, and surface or viaduct along İvedik Caddesi, past the Karşıyaka cemetery and on the Keçiören ring roads. Atapark sits in the middle of its dense neighbourhood, about 1.2 km in a straight line and about 1.5 km on foot from M2's Ovacık station, so it serves the neighbourhood rather than being a change point; M9 meets M2 at Şehitler and M6 at Ovacık; Teknopark Ankara sits at the İvedik OSB interchange with M7 and M8.
  • On M4, Alsancak and Ahimesut sit at their neighbourhoods in the order Bağlıca – Alsancak – Ahimesut, and the station formerly called Ahi Evran is named Elvan; Ahi Evran is now the Sincan light rail stop in Sincan's Ahi Evran neighbourhood. Ahmet Taner Kışlalı is at Sarnıç AVM, and Yaşamkent Siteler about 1.8 km south of Yaşamkent Merkez among the new housing on the way to Dodurga. M6 ends at Karakaya, on Bağlum Bulvarı, rather than going on to Bağlum. The Elvankent stop is placed from its name and could move by up to about 1 km.
  • The Altındağ light rail is a closed loop: Siteler – Ulubey – Karapürçek – Altınköy – Başpınar – Hacı Bayram Veli – Yıldıztepe – Güneşevler–Solfasol – Seyfi Demirsoy – Siteler, 18.5 km, with trains running round it both ways, so no stop is more than half the loop from Siteler. It meets M3 at Siteler and Güneşevler–Solfasol and M9 at Başpınar and Yıldıztepe.
  • The Çayyolu light rail (T4) starts at Ümitköy, the change to M1 and M5, and runs past Galleria, through Çayyolu and along Bangabandhu Bulvarı to Ahmet Taner Kışlalı and Dora Park, then down Alacaatlı Caddesi to Alacaatlı, Alacaatlı Kümeevler and Dodurga: 9.9 km in about 16 minutes, with about 41,000 boardings a day. Starting at Ümitköy rather than Koru puts inner Çayyolu within a short walk of a stop and gives a change to two metro lines; Koru is about 600 m from Bangabandhu.
  • The Akyurt light rail (T9) leaves M3 at Fuar and runs along the Çankırı road (Çankırı Bulvarı), 13.1 km in about 15 minutes: Akyurt Sanayi sits in the factory belt around Güzelhisar and Çınar, Büğdüz at the Büğdüz junction, 1–2 km south of the village, and Akyurt on the main road between Beyazıt and Yıldırım. Akyurt's Sanayi Sitesi, east of the centre, is about 1.8 km beyond the end; a bus lane on the Çankırı road would be the cheaper first step, with the line built in phase 4 if Akyurt's housing keeps growing.
  • The Yapracık light rail (T6) leaves M4 at Bağlıca and follows Bağlıca Bulvarı west through Yeni Bağlıca, about 26,000 residents now 1.4 km from the nearest station, past OYAK's 6,214 new homes for about 25,000 people and Yapracık Köyü to Atayurt. It then turns along Mimar Sinan Caddesi through Yukarıyurtçu to Çankaya University's main campus, about 7,800 students, and crosses the Eskişehir road to the TOKİ homes of Turkuaz: 20.1 km in about 24 minutes, with single vehicles every 5 minutes. It assumes high growth, with OYAK's homes full and Turkuaz at about 28,000 residents. It opens in phase 3; until then buses link the area to Bağlıca.
  • The Etlik–Mamak tram (T2) is an inner-city street tram that links three metro corridors which otherwise meet only in the centre. From Etlik (M6, M7 and the Ring) it runs down Etlik Caddesi past Kasalar, Aşağı Eğlence, Gülhane hospital and Ziraat Fakültesi to Dışkapı (M2), follows Hacı Bayram Bulvarı past Hacı Bayram and Bentderesi to Demirlibahçe (M3 and Başkentray), and continues along Abdülhakhamit Caddesi through Gülveren, Diriliş and MEBS Komutanlığı to Hüseyingazi (M7 and the Ring) and on by Harman to Mamak: 12.8 km in about 32 minutes, with single vehicles every 4 minutes. Running on the surface, it needs no deep excavation in the old town. On the narrower stretches of Etlik Caddesi it shares the street with traffic, which limits its speed. It opens in phase 2.
  • The Millî Kütüphane–Kuyubaşı tram (T7) is a north–south street tram west of the centre that crosses seven rail corridors. From Millî Kütüphane (M1) it runs through Akdeniz Caddesi, Anıt Meydanı and Yukarı Bahçelievler to Beşevler (Ankaray), passes Gazi Üniversitesi to Hipodrom (Başkentray) and Akköprü (M1, M6), continues by Varlık to a stop on the east side of Etlik Şehir Hastanesi, a short walk from the M6 station, and meets the Etlik–Mamak tram at Kasalar. It then follows Tevfik Sağlam Caddesi through Etlik Meydanı to İncirli (M7 and the Ring), takes Ahmet Şefik Kolaylı Caddesi from Esertepe, turns east along Özgürlük Caddesi through Dumlupınar and 19 Mayıs, and passes Dalgıç to Tepebaşı, beside the Cumhuriyet gondola station, before ending by Yakacık at Kuyubaşı (M2, M3 and the Airport Express): 14.4 km in about 36 minutes, with single vehicles every 3 minutes. At that frequency it needs signal priority and its own lanes on most of the route. It opens in phase 3.
  • M2 and M6 meet at Forum, where the M2 depot is planned; one shared depot would save land and cost.
  • A full ring loop runs in 85 minutes and is timetabled at 88, a multiple of the 4-minute pattern, so ring trains never wait at Batıkent or Mamak. Each needs two island platforms laid out by direction, with the flying junctions on the approaches.
  • M3's 140 km/h outer section needs curves of about 1,500 m radius and airport-style trains; it could open at 120 km/h.
  • The Kazan Soda link is being built for freight and needs a passenger station and a shared timetable. The Elmadağ section needs passing loops, a connection at Kayaş and new platforms.
  • Akıncı station sits beside a military air base and needs Defence Ministry agreement; Sincan Cezaevi could open later.
  • The Gölbaşı and Çubuk light rail lines depend on development that the districts' trends do not yet show; safeguarding their corridors now keeps the option at low cost, and the Gölbaşı light rail could open first to ASELSAN. The Çubuk light rail, studied by the Ministry as a 15.84 km light rail along the Çubuk road, needs a timed same-platform change with M3 at Yıldırım Beyazıt Ü., laid out so M3 trains could run through later if Çubuk and Esenboğa grow faster than planned.
  • M2's southern section is being prepared for tender [5], and opening M4 by 2032 needs a tender by 2027–28.

Hills and gradients

Ankara sits in a basin ringed by hills. Kızılay is at about 870 m, the ministries and parliament at 885–900 m, and the castle hill reaches 980 m [17]. The land falls gently west to Etimesgut (about 843 m) and Sincan (about 789 m), and rises in every other direction: to about 950 m in Keçiören, 900–1,020 m in the Mamak valley, about 1,050 m on the Ovacık–Ayvalı heights north of Şentepe, about 1,080 m on the Çankaya hills and about 1,200 m on the plateau around İncek and TED Koleji, before falling again to Gölbaşı (about 970 m) and Lake Mogan (972 m) [17]. On M8, Söğütözü at about 870 m and TED Koleji at about 1,200 m are 11 km apart.

What trains can climb

  • Steel-wheel metro. Usually up to about 4%, with every axle powered, and a little more on short stretches [18]. M1–M9 and the Ring are built to this.
  • Light rail. Grades of 5% are common, and short stretches go steeper [18], so light rail can follow streets that a metro would have to tunnel under.
  • Rubber-tyred metro. Tyres grip better than steel wheels, so these trains climb steeper grades [18]: Lausanne's M2 climbs 338 m in 5.9 km, 5.7% on average and up to 12% [18]. It is the fallback if M8's descent to Gölbaşı cannot be lengthened, at the cost of a separate fleet and depot.
  • Rack. Lyon's line C uses a rack for grades of up to 17% [18].
  • Funicular and gondola. Cable systems climb hills that trains cannot: Istanbul's F1 up to 22%, Haifa's Carmelit 274 m underground, and Medellín's Metrocable gondolas feed its metro from hillside districts [18].

How the plan deals with them

  • Follow the land. Where it can, the network runs along valleys and ridges rather than across them: M1, M4 and the Sincan light rail on the western plain, M7 along the Şentepe–Etlik heights and M5 along the Mamak valley floor.
  • Use tunnel depth. Tunnels run deep under ridges and shallow in valleys, so the track climbs more evenly than the street above, and stations sit on short level stretches so that trains never start uphill.
  • Deep stations, quick to reach. Deep stations such as TED Koleji, Şentepe and those under the Çankaya hills need high-capacity lifts as well as escalators. Kyiv's Arsenalna, 105 m down, shows the cost of escalators alone: the ride takes up to five minutes [18].
  • Cable where trains cannot go. The funicular climbs Çankaya hill to M2, and the gondolas bring Şentepe and Keçiören's hills down to M7 and M2, much as Medellín's do [18].
  • Powerful trains that recover energy. Every axle is powered for the climbs. Lifting a full 6-car train the 330 m from Söğütözü to TED Koleji takes about 270 kWh, and regenerative braking recovers much of it on the way down.
  • Winter. Light rail on slopes needs sanders and heated points, and the gondolas slow down or stop in strong wind; the metro, below ground, is not affected.
  • Time and cost. Climbs slow trains slightly: the 8% margin in the run times should cover them, though M8 between Söğütözü and Gölbaşı may need about a minute more. Deep stations and longer tunnels cost more, and the cost range does not price them separately.
